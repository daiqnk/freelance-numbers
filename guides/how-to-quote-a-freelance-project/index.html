How to Quote a Freelance Project: Phases, Buffer, Fees and a Floor Check

Updated 16 October 2026 · Written with AI assistance. Facts are linked to their sources. How we make content

A fixed-price quote is a bet on your own estimate. This guide shows how to break a project into phases, add a contingency you can defend, decide who pays the payment fees, and check that the final number still clears your hourly floor.

On this page
  1. Break the work into phases
  2. Estimate each phase in hours
  3. Add a contingency buffer
  4. Decide who pays the payment fees
  5. Check the quote against your floor
  6. Write the quote so it protects you

1. Break the work into phases

Clients find one big number hard to judge, and so do you. Split the job into the stages you'd actually work through, each ending in something the client can see, such as a brief, a first draft or a delivered file. Typical phases look like:

  • Discovery and brief (calls, questions, written summary of scope)
  • First draft or design
  • Production or build
  • Revisions and handover

Phases also give you natural points for staged payments and for saying "that's a new phase" when the scope grows.

2. Estimate each phase in hours

Even on a fixed-price job, estimate in hours first. A few habits make estimates more reliable:

  • Use your own history. If you track time, look at how long similar work really took, not how long you remember it taking.
  • Estimate a range, then pick a number. Writing "8–14 hours" makes you think about what could go wrong. Quote close to the upper half unless the work is very routine for you.
  • Count the hidden work. Calls, emails, file preparation, exporting and uploading all take hours.
  • Price revisions explicitly. Decide how many revision rounds are included and estimate them as their own phase.

3. Add a contingency buffer

Most estimates are optimistic. A contingency of, say, 10–25% covers small surprises without renegotiating. That range is a rule of thumb, not a standard. Use less for work you've done many times and more for unfamiliar work or unclear briefs. Two points matter:

  • The buffer is for unknowns inside the agreed scope. New requests are a change of scope and get their own quote.
  • You can show the buffer as a line ("contingency") or fold it into the phase prices. Showing it can make the quote feel more honest. Folding it in avoids a negotiation over that one line. Either is fine as long as you're consistent.

4. Decide who pays the payment fees

If the client pays by card, PayPal or another processor, or in a foreign currency, part of the invoice never reaches you. You have two options:

  • Absorb the fees. They're a cost of doing business. Your rate floor should already include them.
  • Pass them on. Invoice a slightly higher amount so that after fees you receive the full project price. To receive amount A when the provider charges p% + a fixed fee f, invoice (A + f) ÷ (1 − p%).
Some payment providers restrict surcharging, and so do some laws. If you pass fees on, check your provider's terms and local rules first, and state the processing amount clearly on the quote rather than hiding it.

5. Check the quote against your floor

Here's an illustrative project. The numbers are invented and aren't a market rate:

PhaseHours
Discovery & brief4
Design / first draft12
Build / production20
Revisions & handover6
Total42
  • 42 hours × $90 = $3,780, plus a 15% contingency ($567), gives a project total of $4,347.
  • If the client pays with a method costing 3% + $0.30 and you absorb it, about $130.71 goes to fees and $4,216.29 reaches you.
  • That works out to $100.39 per hour if the 42-hour estimate holds, and $87.29 per hour if you use the whole buffer (48.3 hours).
  • Against a floor of $75.42/hour (from the hourly rate guide) the quote passes either way. If the worst-case figure fell below the floor, you'd raise the price, cut scope, or pass the fees on (invoicing $4,481.75 here would land the full $4,347).

6. Write the quote so it protects you

A good quote is short, specific and hard to misread. Include:

  • Scope: what's included, phase by phase, and what is explicitly not included.
  • Revisions: how many rounds are included, and the rate for extra rounds.
  • Timeline and dependencies: dates assume the client sends feedback and materials on time.
  • Payment schedule: for example a deposit to start and the balance on delivery, plus your payment terms in days.
  • Change requests: new work is quoted separately before you start it.
  • Validity: how long the price stands (for example 30 days).
This is general information, not legal or financial advice. For contracts, especially large ones or ones with cross-border clients, consider having a professional review your terms.
